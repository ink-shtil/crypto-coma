\section{The legend, or the statement of the problem}

One summer evening two boys sat on a bench, looked at the stars, and argued about how
many atoms would fit in the Universe.

Had they been born among the Pirah\~a of the Amazon, the argument would have ended quickly.
Pirah\~a, many linguists believe, has no numbers at all --- only ``a little'', ``a bit
more'' and ``more still''\footnote{Everett~\cite{everett2005}; Frank et al.~\cite{frank2008}
showed that even the Pirah\~a ``one'' means something closer to ``a small amount''. A
similarly sparse counting vocabulary is reported for the Munduruk\'u (exact words up to
about five~\cite{pica2004}) and the Warlpiri (``one'', ``two'', ``many''~\cite{butterworth2008})
--- which, notably, does not stop their children from handling exact quantities.}. At best, the boys would have discovered a sort of
``four'' that night. But they grew up where big numbers have names.

And they named ever larger numbers --- a million, a billion, a trillion --- until they
reached an \emph{undecillion}, \(10^{36}\). Even that felt too small.

So they did what any mathematician does when faced with a number that has no name yet:
they gave it one. They made the very first step a large one: let
\(a = \avalue\) --- an undecillion raised to itself. And from there --- upward: each next
number is the previous one raised to itself. Thus was born the series that leads to the
\textbf{crypto coma}.
