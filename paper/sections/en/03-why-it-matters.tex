\section{Why large numbers matter}

The naive dream of ``inventing the largest number'' turns out, on inspection, to be a
thoroughly mathematical one. Here are the arguments that show this childish idea in a
favourable light.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Naming the unsurveyable is what mathematics does.} A googol, a googolplex,
  Graham's number, \(\mathrm{TREE}(3)\) --- mathematics constantly assigns compact names and
  symbols to finite quantities that no one could ever write out. Inventing a symbol for a
  huge number is a mathematician's reflex, not a child's whim.
  \item \textbf{The children rediscovered tetration.} The rule \(L^{L}\) is tetration, and
  the ladder \(a \dots z\) is a fast-growing hierarchy, kin to the Ackermann function,
  Knuth's up-arrows, and the fast-growing hierarchies of proof theory.
  \item \textbf{A named ladder is a scaffold for intuition.} Marked off in steps
  \(a \dots z\), it gives a sense of scale that a single monstrous number cannot.
  \item \textbf{It is a gateway.} The playground game ``what's the biggest number?'' is,
  for many, the first real taste of mathematics. It is to that game that this work is
  dedicated.
\end{enumerate}
